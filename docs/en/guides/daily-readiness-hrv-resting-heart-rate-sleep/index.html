Daily readiness: what HRV, resting heart rate and sleep tell you

Form and fitness say what you have done. Daily readiness says how your body is coping today. Four signals help, each compared with your own values.

Updated: October 6, 2026 · 7 min read

Why comparing with yourself counts

HRV and resting heart rate are highly individual. An HRV of 45 ms can be normal for one person and a warning for another. That is why population values are of little use. What carries meaning is the deviation from your own normal range of the last days.

The four signals

  • HRV (heart rate variability): at least 10 % below the mean of the last seven days is a negative signal; at least 10 % above is a positive one. At least three days of comparison are needed.
  • Resting heart rate: at least 5 beats above the mean of the last seven days is a negative signal, at least 5 below a positive one.
  • Sleep: under 6 hours counts as too short regardless of your average. At least 20 % below your 28-day average is also negative (from 10 nights of history). From 7 hours, sleep counts as restful.
  • Wellbeing: your own rating on a scale of 1 to 5. Up to 2 is negative, 4 and above positive.

How a verdict emerges

Two or more negative signals mean limited readiness. No negative and at least one positive signal mean good readiness. Anything in between is normal. If a signal is missing, for example because you do not wear your watch at night, it simply drops out. Nothing is estimated.

What to do with it

  • A single bad signal is normal. One short night or a glass of wine in the evening is enough.
  • Several negative signals over several days, plus high fatigue, are a good reason to move a hard session or ride easy.
  • Heat, illness, stress and alcohol change HRV and resting heart rate strongly. The numbers do not say why.
Daily readiness is a training hint, not a medical statement. If values stay unusual or you have symptoms, talk to your doctor.
